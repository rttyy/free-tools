Core Optical Proofing Concepts & Worked Examples
1. Why do my prints look different from what I see on my computer monitor? ▼

Computer monitors emit direct light via LEDs/backlights (transmissive luminance), often set between 150 to 350 cd/m², achieving contrast ratios over 1000:1. Prints are reflective media: they produce light only by reflecting ambient room lighting. Under typical indoor light (100 lux), paper white reflects only ~26 cd/m², creating a severe luminance mismatch (up to -3.5 EV). Additionally, ink physical density limits maximum black (Dmax), reducing print contrast range to between 15:1 (matte) and 140:1 (glossy).

WORKED EXAMPLE: Inputs: • Monitor Brightness: 220 cd/m² • Room Ambient Light: 100 lux (2700K Warm Tungsten) • Substrate: Fine Art Cotton Matte (Dmax 1.40, Reflectance 82%) Outputs & Math: • Paper White Luminance = (100 lux * 0.82) / π = 26.1 cd/m² • Luminance Mismatch EV = log2(26.1 / 220) = -3.07 EV (Print appears ~3 stops darker) • Paper Black Floor = (100 lux * 10^-1.40) / π = 1.04 cd/m² (Monitor black is 0.22 cd/m²) • Dynamic Range Reduction: 1000:1 (9.97 stops) -> 25:1 (4.65 stops) • Out-of-Gamut Clipping: Saturated cyans & deep reds lose 24.3% volume.
2. Why is 18% grey considered to be in the middle for photography? ▼

Human visual perception of lightness is non-linear and follows a cube-root power law (CIE L* scale). A object with 18% physical linear reflectance (Y = 0.18 relative to 1.0 white) is perceived by human eyes as exactly half-way between black and white (L* = 50). On matte papers with reduced Dmax, the effective black floor lifts, causing linear 18% gray to drop to perceived lightness L* ~41, requiring midtone boost compensation for accurate printing.

WORKED EXAMPLE: Inputs: • Linear Reflectance: Y = 0.18 (relative to reference white Y_n = 1.00) • CIE L* Lightness Formula: L* = 116 * (Y/Y_n)^(1/3) - 16 Math Demonstration: • Standard Midpoint: L* = 116 * (0.18)^(1/3) - 16 = 116 * (0.5646) - 16 = 49.5% (50% perceptual midpoint). • Paper Compression (Matte Dmax 1.35, Min Reflectance 4.5%): The effective perceived midpoint shifts to 22.8% linear reflectance to feel 'halfway' between paper black and paper white.
3. Aren't all digital images ultimately just pixel values between 0 - 255? ▼

Digital 8-bit integer RGB values (0–255) are device-dependent relative numbers, not absolute color measurements. An RGB value of (255, 0, 0) specifies "maximum red drive signal" for a specific display device. The actual physical color produced (CIE xy chromaticity, absolute nits, and Lab color coordinates) depends entirely on display backlight brightness, working color space gamut primaries, and physical ink limits (Dmax & CMYK gamut boundary).

WORKED EXAMPLE: Inputs: • Pixel RGB Value: (255, 0, 0) [Pure Red] • Display: 200 cd/m² calibrated monitor Transformation & Mapping: • On Monitor: Emits 42.5 cd/m² physical red light at chromaticity xy = (0.64, 0.33), CIE Lab(53, 80, 67). • On CMYK Glossy Paper: Maximum achievable red ink is Lab(48, 65, 48). • Truncation: 8-bit RGB (255,0,0) clips to CMYK equivalent (0, 92, 86, 0). Pixel integers only gain physical meaning when bound to specific optical targets.

Frequently asked questions

Why do my prints look different from what I see on my computer monitor?

Monitors emit light (backlit) and have a dynamic range often exceeding 1000:1, while prints reflect light and rarely exceed a 100:1 contrast ratio. If your monitor is set too bright for your room, your eyes adjust to the screen, making the physical print appear dim and muddy by comparison.

Why is 18% grey considered to be in the middle for photography?

Human vision is non-linear; we are more sensitive to changes in shadows than in highlights. While 18% reflectance is mathematically low, our brains perceive it as the geometric mean between black and white, making it the visual 'middle' point for exposure.

Aren't all digital images ultimately just pixel values between 0 - 255?

Those numbers are just coordinates within a specific color space (like sRGB or ProPhoto). A value of 255 red in sRGB is a different physical color than 255 red in AdobeRGB; without a defined color space and calibrated output, those numbers don't represent absolute light.

What do Hue, Chroma, Saturation, Value, Tones, Tints, and Shades mean?

Hue is the color family (red, blue). Chroma is the purity or intensity of a color. Saturation is chroma relative to brightness. Value is the lightness/darkness. Tints are colors mixed with white, Shades are mixed with black, and Tones are mixed with gray.
