About this free tool

The Night Sky Exposure & Color Signal Lab is a free browser-based optical calculator designed for nightscape photographers shooting without equatorial mounts. It combines sensor-specific NPF calculations with light pollution modeling to maximize your raw color signal while preventing star elongation. An optional single-file HTML download is available for field use completely offline.

How to use it

  1. Select your camera body or enter your sensor's exact pixel pitch in microns.
  2. Input your lens focal length, maximum practical aperture, and target sky declination.
  3. Set your shooting location's Bortle class to evaluate skyglow background flux against target signal.
  4. Review the recommended NPF exposure ceiling, entrance pupil score, and expected SNR.
  5. Dial the calculated shutter, aperture, and ISO into manual mode on your camera.

Frequently asked questions

How to have colors in Milky-way?

Milky Way colors—such as the reddish hydrogen-alpha emission regions, yellow-orange galactic core, and blue-white stars—are real physical phenomena obscured by sensor noise when exposures are too short or apertures too small. To reveal them, maximize your lens entrance pupil (focal length divided by f-number) and expose to the exact limit calculated by the NPF rule without trailing. Stacking multiple identical exposures further suppresses random chrominance noise, uncovering true astronomical color before grading.

What tips and advice do you have for photographing the Aurora Borealis?

Unlike the static Milky Way, auroral curtains move dynamically, meaning long exposures turn vibrant bands into a blurry, washed-out green haze. Prioritize shutter speeds between 1 and 6 seconds by opening your lens to its widest aperture (f/1.4 to f/2.8) and raising your ISO to 1600–6400 depending on your sensor's read noise. Use daylight white balance (around 5500K) to preserve accurate purples, reds, and greens, and monitor your histogram to prevent blowing out the green channel during active substorms.

Photo of sprites in a clear dark sky, is this possible?

Yes, photographing red sprites against a clear, dark sky is entirely possible, but requires an unobstructed line of sight to a distant mesospheric thunderstorm 100 to 300 miles (160 to 500 km) away. Because sprites occur 50 to 90 kilometers above ground level, the storm itself must be well below your local horizon so dense clouds do not obscure the upper atmosphere. Use a fast prime lens (50mm or 85mm at f/1.4 to f/1.8), continuous high-speed interval shooting, and high ISO settings aimed just above the distant horizon under clear overhead skies.

Why (actually) is the night sky so bright in the city? How far up is that happening?

Urban skyglow is caused by artificial upward light scattering off gas molecules (Rayleigh scattering) and suspended aerosol particulates like smog, fog, and dust (Mie scattering). This scattering primarily occurs in the troposphere, concentrated within the planetary boundary layer from ground level up to roughly 2 to 3 kilometers (6,500 to 10,000 feet), though faint scattered photons reach altitudes of 10 to 15 kilometers. The scattered artificial light creates an elevated background photon flux that overpowers faint celestial signal before it can reach your camera's sensor.
