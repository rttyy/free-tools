About this free tool

The Macro Photography Extension Tube & Magnification Lab is a browser-based optical simulator designed for macro, product, and nature photographers. It models multi-tube stacking sequences, calculates light loss and effective apertures, and plots Airy disk diffraction against your camera sensor's pixel pitch. Use the free online bench to plan working distance, or grab the offline pack for field reference.

How to use it

  1. Select your camera sensor format to load your sensor's exact pixel pitch.
  2. Input your lens focal length, native minimum focus distance, and maximum reproduction ratio.
  3. Stack individual extension tube sizes (e.g., 12mm, 20mm, 36mm) in the simulator.
  4. Review calculated working distance, effective aperture, EV compensation, and Airy disk limits.

Frequently asked questions

How can I calculate what the effect of an extension tube will be?

The general formula is New Magnification = Native Magnification + (Extension Tube Length / Lens Focal Length). For example, adding a 25mm tube to a 50mm lens with a native 0.15x magnification yields approximately 0.15 + (25 / 50) = 0.65x magnification. Note that this formula assumes a simple lens design; internal focusing lenses will vary slightly.

How does a teleconverter affect depth of field?

A teleconverter magnifies the image produced by the prime lens without changing the lens's physical distance to the subject. Because depth of field is governed primarily by aperture and reproduction ratio on the sensor, multiplying your focal length increases subject magnification, which proportionally reduces optical depth of field for any given camera position and marked f-stop.

Why isn't this portrait taken at f/29 sharp?

At f/29, optical diffraction severely limits resolution. When an aperture narrows to that degree, light waves bend around the diaphragm edges and spread into Airy disks that are significantly larger than the individual pixels on your camera sensor. Even with a high-end lens, this physical phenomenon blurs fine detail and lowers overall image contrast.

What is the difference between marked f-stop and effective f-stop in macro photography?

Marked f-stop is the aperture ratio set on your lens barrel or camera dial when focused at infinity. Effective f-stop accounts for the physical distance between the rear lens element and the sensor as you focus closer or add extension tubes ($f_{eff} \approx f_{marked} \times [1 + m]$). At 1:1 magnification, an aperture set to f/8 transmits light and creates diffraction equivalent to f/16.
